# NENYI — money taken from a classmate (16/11/26)

> **Reported:** H, 16/11/26. Younger son (Nenyi, ~10, Kansview Montessori, Oyaripa) **allegedly** took/took money belonging to a fellow pupil.
> **Already happened:** Mum reprimanded him **by phone**.
> **H's side note:** he likes drawing and draws at any given moment, especially with a **biro pen**.
> **Status:** open — no admission recorded, no facts established. H wants to understand *why*.

---

## Grounding from his own records (not speculation)

- **Drawing is a documented interest and a competence zone.** `care/ACTIVITY_PLANS.md` lists Nenyi as *"drawing, reading aloud, timer-keeper, building confidence/independence."* He draws scenes from reading, draws "blueprints" before builds. `DR_UMAR_DISCIPLINE_FRAMEWORK.md` even has a **"gallery curator"** privilege — drawings on the wall.
- **He has a real expressive-language gap.** School observation: sentences sometimes incomplete, speech too soft, **/s/ for /sh/** substitution. Monthly speech therapy. This is the single most important fact for interpreting the incident.
- **He is described as "younger but can articulate"** in the discipline framework — so he *can* talk, but under stress and by phone that ability collapses.
- **The family's own discipline model already exists:** `DR_UMAR_DISCIPLINE_FRAMEWORK.md` — rewards & consequences, privilege-based, **restorative, no lecture mid-conflict**, later conversation: *"What happened? What could you do differently?"* Use it. Do not invent a new regime.

## Why this could be happening — ranked, each testable

### 1. Drawing materials ran out (most likely, most fixable)
He draws **constantly, with a biro**. Biros die fast, get lent, lost or confiscated. If his pen finished mid-morning and he had no money and no easy way to ask, taking a few cedis to buy one is the **shortest path between a want and a need** — not malice.
**Test:** Does he have working pens right now? Has he asked for pens recently? What does he normally do when one finishes?

### 2. No chop money / not enough, while peers have it
Break-time food, sachet water, the school shop. If classmates buy and he can't, the pressure is **structural, not moral**. Hunger plus visibility of others buying is a classic driver.
**Test:** Does he get pocket money? How much? Is it enough for water and a snack? Does he compare himself to classmates?

### 3. The language gap makes *asking* expensive
A child who finds it hard to form sentences and whose speech is soft will often **act instead of ask**. Asking requires words, timing, and the nerve to be heard. Taking is silent.
→ This is probably the deepest driver, and it connects directly to 1 and 2.

### 4. Money as social currency — buying belonging
A 10-year-old with speech difficulty may use treats/gifts to hold friendships. Spending to be included.

### 5. Coercion or extortion by an older child ⚠️ RULE OUT
If someone demanded money from him, taking it may have been **fear-driven, not greed**. This is the one that must not be missed.
**Test:** sudden reluctance to go to school, missing items, fear, unexplained need for money, an older child's name recurring.

### 6. Copying or a dare
Another child did it and it looked normal, or a group dared him.

### 7. **It may not be what it looks like** — the word "allegedly" is doing real work
At 10, ownership boundaries are still forming: money **found**, money **given to hold**, or money **planted** in a bag all read as "he took it". And a child with a language delay is a **poor self-defender and an easy person to blame** — he may not have the vocabulary to explain himself or to push back.
**Do not treat this as established fact until he has had a chance to say what happened in a way he can actually manage.**

## What a 10-year-old actually needs money for in school

Practical, concrete — check each:
- **Chop money** — break-time food, and whether it's enough
- **Water** — sachet/ice water; it is hot and he is a walking, drawing child
- **Biro pens, pencils, eraser, sharpener** — *the live hypothesis*
- **Exercise books / drawing paper**
- **School-shop items** — stickers, marbles, cards, the yard economy
- **Treats to share** — social currency
- **Being pressured for it** — the coercive case

## What the phone reprimand probably achieved

Compliance, not understanding. Long-distance on a phone:
- He could not explain himself — his language is weakest exactly under stress.
- Shame without a route to repair teaches **hiding better**, not honesty.
- It may have closed the channel you now need open.
→ Do not relitigate by phone. The real conversation is **in person, calm, once**.

## The plan (aligned with the Dr Umar framework already in use)

### Step 1 — Settle the money first, privately and without drama
Repay the other child's family **regardless of what is still unclear**. H covers the loss. This is the integrity move, it protects the school relationship, and it removes the debt from the emotional conversation. Then find out the truth at your own pace.

### Step 2 — Ask the school what they actually saw
Ask the class teacher privately, neutrally:
- Who reported it? Was it **witnessed** or inferred?
- Is there any chance he was **put up to it**, or that it was another child?
- Is an **older child** involved?
Do not accept "he did it" without a witness. Also do not defend him blindly — just get the facts.

### Step 3 — The restorative conversation, in person
Their own model, not an interrogation:
> "What happened at break time?" → let him talk, don't fill the silence → "What could you do differently?" → "What needs to happen to put it right?"

**Not** "why did you steal?" — that's an accusation dressed as a question, and it invites a lie.

### Step 4 — Use the drawing. This is the key move.
He is a drawer who draws at any given moment. **A child with expressive-language difficulty can draw what he cannot say.**
> "Draw what happened at break time."

Then let him narrate the drawing in his own time. This bypasses the exact channel that fails him — his speech — and uses the one that works. It also calms him. This is likely to tell you more than an hour of questioning.

### Step 5 — Close the structural gap so it can't recur
This is the part that actually prevents a repeat:
- **A small, defined weekly allowance** — so he is never improvising. He needs to be able to buy water, food, and a pen without asking.
- **A stocked drawing kit** — biros, pencils, sharpener, exercise/drawing book. Replenished on a schedule, kept in his bag. If the hypothesis is right, this alone removes the motive.
- **A standing rule, said out loud:** *"If you need something, you tell me, Mum, or your teacher. Asking is never a punishment."* Make asking cheaper than taking.
- **Sanction drawing properly** — the "gallery curator" privilege already exists: his drawings on the wall. Give him a legitimate stage.

### Step 6 — The consequence, chosen carefully
Per the framework, privilege-based and reparative, not humiliating:
- He **repays** from his own money or earns it back through a chore.
- Suspend a **different** privilege (e.g. screen time).
- **Do NOT make drawing the punishment.** It is his regulation tool, his confidence zone, and — if the hypothesis is right — the very thing the money was for. Turning it into a punishment would remove his main coping mechanism and confirm that his gift is a problem.

### Step 7 — Watch, don't hover
For 2–3 weeks note: pens in the bag, sudden reluctance to go to school, missing belongings, unexplained money, new names of older children, mood after school.

## Open questions for H (the facts I don't have)

1. **Did he admit it**, or is it the other child's word? Was there a **witness**?
2. **How much money**, and where was it kept?
3. **Does he get any pocket/break money** — and how much?
4. **Who told Mum** — the school, or the other parent?
5. Is he **still going to school happily**, or has anything changed since?

*None of this is a diagnosis. At 10, taking money is overwhelmingly **an unmet want + low impulse control + opportunity**, not a character trait — it is highly responsive to how the adults respond now.*
