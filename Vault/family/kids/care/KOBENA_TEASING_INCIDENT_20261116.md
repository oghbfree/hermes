## What actually happened

- **H has said "big belly kobena" to Kobena as teasing — "what I used to say"** — and did so again
  yesterday (15/11/26).
- **Today (16/11/26):** Kobena came home **crying and reciting the phrase**. Mum asked why he was
  crying. **Nenyi — who had been with Kobena — simply narrated what he had observed:** that it was
  because H had been saying that.

**Nenyi told the truth when asked. He was a witness, not a perpetrator.**

---

## 1. Nenyi — protect the honesty

This is the part that is easy to get wrong and expensive to get wrong.

If Nenyi is blamed, questioned sharply, or made to feel he caused trouble by answering, he learns the
lesson that matters most here: **telling the truth about an adult gets you hurt.** That is a habit you
do not want forming at 10, in a house where he will one day need to report things that actually matter.

What he needs instead:
- **Thank him for telling the truth** — clearly, once. Praise the *honesty*, not the content:
  "You told the truth when Mum asked. That was right, and you're not in trouble."
- **Do not ask him to soften or retract it**, and don't discuss the incident in front of him as though
  he were the problem.
- **No consequence of any kind attaches to him for this.** He did the correct thing.

---

## 2. Kobena — the distress is real, and the echo is his report

- He came home **reciting** the phrase rather than saying "Dad said I have a big belly." That is
  **delayed echolalia** — replaying heard language, a core feature of how autistic children process
  and communicate. **The words ARE the report.** He likely lacks the language to say "I felt
  humiliated", so the phrase carries it.
- **The phrase is now attached to shame**, and may keep surfacing for days even though nothing fresh
  has happened. Repetition is **not** evidence of a new incident.
- **Don't tell him to stop repeating it.** Suppressing it removes his only channel. Replace the script.
- **Don't discuss his body** — neither reassurance ("you're not big") nor correction. Both keep the
  frame alive. Reassure his **worth**, not his measurements.
- **Don't lead with "it was just a joke" or "I didn't mean it."** Those tell a child his hurt is
  wrong. Name the hurt first: *"That hurt you. You're allowed to be upset."*
- **Give him a replacement line** so the old script has somewhere to go: *"You can say: 'Don't say
  that. It hurts.'"* Practise it once, calmly.

---

## 3. H — the repair has to come from the source

The phrase is H's, so the repair has to be H's. No rule, no lecture and no reassurance from anyone
else substitutes for it.

**A specific apology, short and unqualified:**
> "I say 'big belly kobena' and that was wrong. I hurt you and I'm sorry. I won't say it again."

Rules for it:
- **Name the exact words.** Not "sorry about yesterday" — the phrase itself, so he knows precisely
  what is being withdrawn.
- **No "but."** No "but I was only playing", no "but you know I love you." A qualifier turns an
  apology into a defence.
- **Don't ask him to reassure you.** He is not required to say "it's okay". Let him receive it.
- **Don't demand forgiveness or a smile.** He may not be ready, and that's allowed.
- **Don't over-explain how it was meant.** Intent is not the issue; impact is.

Then close the loop by **not doing it again** — the only proof that lands with him.

**On intent vs impact.** It was almost certainly fond ribbing — the kind that passes between adults,
which is why it survived as a habit. That is exactly why it did damage: to an 11-year-old with ASD,
about his body, from the person he looks up to, **affectionate tone does not soften it — it makes it
confusing**, because warmth and humiliation arrive together. That combination is harder to process
than plain unkindness.

**Because this is a long-standing habit rather than a slip, it has to be actively retired.** The
phrase will want to come out automatically. Expect to catch yourself — and if you slip, a plain
apology the first time is worth far more than pretending it didn't happen.

---

## 4. The household rule

> **"We don't comment on people's bodies in this house — not as a joke, not affectionately, not ever."**

Apply it to the adults first. A rule that only binds the children isn't a rule; it's a message about
who is allowed to do what. If Mum, relatives or visitors use similar teasing, it needs the same answer.

---

## 5. Watch for

- The phrase intruding at **other times** (bedtime, mealtimes, unprompted) — suggests it has become
  intrusive rather than residual.
- **Food refusal, hiding his body, avoiding changing in front of others, reluctance for school.**
- Any **new self-directed comment** about his body — if he starts saying it about himself, it has
  internalised, and that is the signal to involve his care team.
